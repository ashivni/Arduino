01

How a breadboard works

A breadboard is a grid of holes with hidden metal clips underneath. Push two wires into holes that share a clip and they are connected — no solder, no heat, nothing permanent.

Every hole looks the same from the top, which is exactly the problem. The whole skill is knowing which holes are secretly wired together. There are only two rules, and the coloured lines printed on the plastic tell you both.

The power rails run vertically. Down each side of the board are two long columns marked with a red line (+) and a blue or black line (−). Every hole in one of those columns is connected to every other hole in the same column, from top to bottom. That is one continuous piece of metal. You feed your battery or your Arduino's power into these, once, and the whole board can reach it.

The component rows run horizontally. The big middle area is split into short rows of five holes. Each group of five is connected across, and connected to nothing else. Put a resistor leg in one of those five holes and a wire in another, and they are joined.

The channel down the middle breaks everything. The trench splitting the board in half is not decoration. A row on the left half is a completely separate node from the row on the right half, even though they look like one line. That gap exists so chips can straddle it with each leg landing in its own row.

The reason any of this works is a strip of springy metal hiding under each group of holes. Push a wire in and the clip grabs it; push a second wire into a hole above the same clip and the two are touching metal-to-metal.

Bench habit

Before you power anything up, say the circuit out loud, hole by hole: "5 V rail, to A7, resistor A7 to A12, LED A12 to A15, wire A15 to ground rail." Nine times out of ten, a circuit that doesn't work has two legs in the same five-hole row that were supposed to be in different ones.

02

Three components, three symbols

Circuit diagrams don't draw what parts look like — they draw what parts do. Three symbols get you through this whole chapter.

Battery — the pump

A battery does one job: it holds one terminal at a higher voltage than the other. Voltage is pressure. Current — the actual flow of charge — leaves the + terminal, travels the whole loop, and returns to −. If the loop is broken anywhere, nothing flows anywhere. There is no such thing as current going halfway.

In this chapter your battery is the Arduino. Its 5V pin is the + terminal and its GND pin is the − terminal.

Resistor — the tap

A resistor restricts how much current gets through for a given voltage. That relationship is Ohm's law:

I = V ÷ Rcurrent (amps) = voltage (volts) ÷ resistance (ohms)

Bigger R, smaller current. A resistor has no polarity, so it cannot be inserted backwards. You read its value off the coloured bands: for the 1 kΩ resistors in your kit, brown, black, red, gold. First band 1, second band 0, third band ×100 — that's 10 × 100 = 1000 Ω. Gold is the tolerance, ±5%.

LED — the one-way lamp

LED stands for Light Emitting Diode, and the diode half matters as much as the light half. A diode is a one-way valve: current passes from anode to cathode and is blocked in the other direction. Put an LED in backwards and it simply stays dark — annoying, not fatal.

Two ways to tell the legs apart on a fresh LED:

  • The longer leg is the anode (+). Mnemonic: longer leg, longer word, positive.
  • The plastic rim has a flat spot on the cathode side (−). Use this once the legs have been trimmed.

Here is the part that gets LEDs killed: an LED does not obey Ohm's law. Once you pass its turn-on voltage — about 2 V for a red one — it barely resists at all, so the current shoots up until something burns. An LED will happily destroy itself in under a second given the chance. It needs a resistor beside it to do the limiting. That is the entire reason for section 4.

03

The pins on an Arduino

The black strips along the edges of the board are headers, and every hole in them is a pin with a name printed next to it. Three of them are all you need today.

GND — the pin everything comes back to

GND is short for ground. It is the − terminal, the zero-volt reference, and the destination for every bit of current that leaves any other pin. Voltage is always a difference, and GND is what the difference is measured against — "5 V" really means "5 volts above GND."

An Uno has three GND pins: two side by side on the POWER header, and one on the digital header between AREF and pin 13. They are all the same piece of copper inside the board, so pick whichever one is nearest your wire. There is no "better" ground.

5V and 3V3 — the two supplies

5V is your workhorse. Whenever the board is powered — over USB or from the barrel jack — this pin sits at a steady 5 volts above GND. It is the pin you will wire to the red rail of your breadboard for the rest of this chapter.

3V3 gives 3.3 volts instead. Plenty of modern sensors and radio modules are damaged by 5 V and need this one. It comes from a small regulator and can only supply about 50 mA, so it is not a general-purpose power source — treat it as a specialist tool.

Never do this

Do not put a wire directly from 5V to GND. That is a short circuit: no resistance in the path, so the current is limited only by what the board can deliver. Best case a protection fuse trips and the board goes dead until you unplug it. Wires get hot fast.

Same rule for the breadboard: never run a jumper straight from the red rail to the blue rail.

04

Lighting one LED

Everything so far comes together in a loop with three parts: a supply, a resistor to limit the current, and an LED to spend it as light.

Read the schematic as a journey. Current leaves 5V at the top, is squeezed through the resistor, passes down through the LED from anode to cathode — making light on the way — and arrives back at GND. One loop, no branches, no dead ends.

Why 1 kΩ, and what it actually buys you

A red LED drops about 2 V across itself once it lights. The supply is 5 V. So the resistor gets whatever's left over:

VR = 5 V − 2 V = 3 Vthe resistor gets whatever the LED doesn't

I = VR ÷ R = 3 V ÷ 1000 Ω = 0.003 A= 3 mA through the whole loop

A typical 5 mm LED is rated for 20 mA and starts being visible around 1 mA. At 3 mA you get a clear, comfortable glow with an enormous safety margin — you would need to be off by a factor of six before anything is at risk. That margin is exactly what you want in a room full of first circuits.

With no resistor at all, the only thing opposing the current is the LED's own tiny internal resistance and the board's limits. Hundreds of milliamps try to flow, and the LED dies — often instantly, sometimes with a small pop.

Building it on the breadboard

Now translate the schematic into holes. Power off — unplug the USB — while you wire, then plug in last.

  1. Red jumper: Arduino 5V → any hole in the + rail.
  2. Black jumper: Arduino GND → any hole in the − rail.
  3. Red jumper: + rail → A7.
  4. Resistor: B7 → B12. Rows 7 and 12 are different nodes, which is what makes it a series component rather than a bridge across the same row.
  5. LED: long leg into C12, short leg into C15.
  6. Black jumper: D15 → − rail.
If it doesn't light

Work the list in order. 1. Is the LED backwards? Pull it out, flip it, push it back — this is the answer most of the time. 2. Are both legs of a component in the same five-hole row? Then it is shorted out. 3. Did a leg land in the channel instead of a hole? 4. Is the board actually powered — is the green ON light lit? 5. Swap in a different LED; occasionally you get a dud.

05

Exercise: light two LEDs

Here is a schematic and nothing else. Your job is to turn it into a working breadboard — no build photo this time.

Both LEDs sit between the same two nodes, 5V and GND, in two separate branches. That arrangement is called parallel. Each branch gets its own 1 kΩ resistor, and each branch carries its own 3 mA independently of the other.

Exercise 1.1 — Two LEDs Working time: 20 min · Pairs

What you need

×1Arduino Uno + USB cable
×1Breadboard
×2LEDs (any colour)
×21 kΩ resistors
brown black red gold
×4Jumper wires

Your steps

  • Plan on paper first. Write the node list before you touch a component — which row each leg goes in, from 5 V all the way back to GND. Show it to me before you build.
  • Wire it with the USB unplugged. Power the rails first (5 V → red rail, GND → blue rail), then build both branches.
  • Give each LED its own resistor. Two LEDs sharing one resistor is a different circuit, and a worse one — the brighter LED steals current from the dimmer one.
  • Keep the branches in different rows. If LED1's anode and LED2's anode land in the same five-hole row, you have joined them by accident.
  • Plug in the USB. Both LEDs should light, equally bright.
  • Test it. Unplug the USB, pull LED1 out of the board, plug back in. LED2 must still light. If it doesn't, your branches aren't parallel — find the shared row.

Check your understanding

  1. How much total current is the Arduino's 5V pin supplying with both LEDs lit? How does that compare to its ~500 mA budget?
  2. You put both LEDs in series instead — 5 V, one resistor, LED1, LED2, GND. Each LED drops 2 V, so how much is left across the resistor, and what current flows? Predict the brightness before you try it.
  3. Someone swaps a 1 kΩ resistor for 10 kΩ on one branch. What changes, and by how much?
  4. What single wire, added anywhere, would turn this into the short circuit from the warning in section 3?

Going further

Finished early? Move the top end of one branch off the 5V pin and onto digital pin 13 instead. That LED is now under software control rather than permanently on — which is exactly where the next chapter starts.
